# Tech Independence

Principles for tech independence:


## Before you choose
- Technology giveth and technology taketh away

If it gives something, it must be taking something away. What is that? Your time, attention, skill, opportunity to do something else, money?

- What is its purpose?
Is there a real need for the thing, or am I buying because I am swayed by marketing? Does it align with my core principles or is it just shiny object? Am I doing to for my own hapiness or to become part of a tribe?

- Before entering, think about the exit.
Is the company giving me an easy button to delete my account and my data? Until this is confirmed, don't use the service (Make a fake account or make a real one, but don't put your data into it.).

- Before using, think about switching.
Is the company giving me a way to switch the service if I don't like their service (Are they giving you an export option in standard, non-proprietary format like Markdown, CSV, JSON?)? And don't forget to read the export format before you commit. The exported data might be broken and will prove to be useless.

Assume the company will disappear tomorrow, and act accordingly.

- Compound
Time and effort invested in a software/service should compound. Learning Git, plain text, CLI, or scripting pays forever. Learning a proprietary UI pays until the redesign or shutdown.

## How to choose

- Local-first software. 
It should work if you are offline or if the company's servers are not working. If the company goes bankrupt, you should be able to switch the local data elsewhere. For multi-device use, you can sync. And take backups.

- Trust the lindy effect. 
The longer a technology has been around, the longer it is likely to stay around. Plain text, email, and RSS have survived decades, and they will probably survive decades more.

- Boring
When in doubt, choose the boring tech over the hype. If the 'dumb' option is enough, don't choose 'smart'. Why does your fridge want to connect to the internet? Such smart options often come with software dependencies, security vulnerabilities, and planned obsolescence.

- Open-source over proprietary
Generally, open-source products don't prioritize profits over customers. (There are some open-source projects that are run by essentially a single company. Those software are open-source on paper but not in reality.)

- Curation over algorithm
Choose what you consume. Curate what you consume. If you depend on algorithms, the companies will manipulate you for their profits. (RSS feed readers are sufficient.)

- Right to repair
Hardware should be repairable. If possible, choose the simple, boring options. Not the smart, high-tech version.

- Know the business model
How is the company benefiting from the service? Direct payments from customers or something else?

- Friction is good
Little friction while using something is good. Friction makes you mindful of what you are doing. Mindfulness tells you what is needful and what is wastage.

- Control your surface area of attack
Minimize account count. Every account is an attack surface—security, cognition, and dependence.

- Prefer composable tools over monoliths. Small tools that do one thing well survive longer and fail cleaner.
- Seek "interoperability" over "ecosystems".

- Whenever possible, choose tools with a community, not just a company or a person. A company can go bankrupt, and a single-person-led software can die tomorrow with that person.

- Tools built for billions often serve no one well. Prefer tools built for humans with taste. Software that "connects everyone" often connects you to no one meaningfully.

- If possible, have separate hardware and software for separate tasks. A "do-everything" device is a "distract-everything" device.

- Prefer tools that respect your attention over those designed to capture it.

- Stay away from walled gardens like Facebook, Instagram, and Reddit. One day, they are going to take away or misuse your data. Still, if you want to use it, be ready to lose your data any day.

- Prefer simple tools you understand over powerful tools you don't. If needed, move gradually from simple to powerful.

## Maintenance

- Keep a technology journal
Log what tools you adopt, why, how they perform over time, and lessons learned to build personal wisdom and avoid repeating mistakes.

- Your toolchain (what toolchain? The word is not clear) should fit in your head. If you need documentation just to remember how your own system works, it’s too complex. (Although you should still document.)

- Not everything needs to be stored. Selective forgetting/deletion is healthy.

- When something breaks, try to fix it yourself before asking for help. The attempt teaches more than the answer. In other words, fuck around and find out.

- Periodically audit and declutter your digital life. If you haven't used a tool in six months, delete it and the data associated with it.

- Every dependency is a liability. Minimize them.

- Design your workflows so that a tool disappearing is annoying, not catastrophic.

- Automation should reduce cognitive load, not replace understanding. So, automate slowly while understanding every step. Never automate in bulk.

## Backup and migration
Use the rule of 3-2-1. 3 copies. 2 formats (hardware and drive). 1 copy offsite. Backups are only real if you've tested restoring from them. Backups must be boring and automatic.

- Have analog fallbacks
If something is super-critical for you, have an analog fallback. Papers work when devices don't. 

- Document your setup and workflows — Keep personal notes on configurations, custom scripts, and migration paths so you (or future you) can rebuild or escape any system quickly.

- Treat sync conflicts as a smell. It's a smoke of your systems under fire.

- Version history is a form of freedom. Undo is power. Make friends with Git.

## Privacy and Security
- Diversify
Never put all your eggs in one basket. Your critical accounts should be with different service providers.

- Encryption is a default, not an option. Prefer vendors that encrypt everything by default and don't hold the keys.

- Never depend on a single vendor for identity. Email, domains, password managers, and 2FA should not all belong to the same company. In this case, the more, the merrier.

- Audit your permissions regularly.

- Know your digital footprint. Have a good grasp of what accounts you have, what type of data and metadata you are creating, and what you are consuming.


## Budget

- Subscription fatigue is a good signal for audit. Count what you're paying monthly and ask if it's worth it. 

- If possible, use an FOSS version. The next choice should be to buy the software. At last, if nothing else is available, subscribe.

- Subscription money silently compounds. You can pay the same money to FOSS projects.
- Wait 24 hours before paying for software or hardware.

- If needed, pay to prevent yourself from ads, growth hacks, data selling, etc.

- Can you use the money in a better place? Is this the best use of this money?


## Your rights as a customer
- Right to repair
- Right to own (renting is understable, but everywhere?)
- Right to be forgotten
- Right to know what is happening under the hood
- Right to have a customer service (if you are paying)
- Right to leave the product
- Right to choose a product from another company that works with the current product

## Mix
- Remember that these tools exist for a purpose. Have a good amount of complete analog hours to keep reminding you that.
- If you understand that technology is giving you value, invest some time and effort to understand it. Otherwise, you'll be vulnerable to manipulation. Develop a habit of giving 3-4 hours a week to learn more about technology.
- If a platform controls who can reach you, you don't control your relationships.
- If possible, work with files, not with software.
- Question default settings. Defaults serve the maker, not necessarily you.
- Opt out first, opt in selectively.
- Learn principles, not just interfaces. Interfaces change, but fundamentals persist.
- Separate creation from consumption. Creation thrives on friction while consumption thrives on its removal.
- Personalization often means behavioral shaping. If a tool asks for personalization, ask why?
- Mastery of a few tools beats shallow use of many.
- Sometimes the right tool is no tool at all.
- If I get something (sofware or hardware), am I willing to denounce something to stay minimalistic.
